From Spatial Structure to Rain-Gauge Network Design

A didactic and operational workflow for process-based site selection (Burgwald)

1 1. Why rainfall network design is not a technical optimisation problem (S0)

Designing a precipitation monitoring network in a complex upland landscape is not a question of geometric coverage or statistical convenience.
It is a scientific design problem that links spatial heterogeneity, process understanding, and observational constraints.

Rainfall variability in low mountain regions emerges from interacting controls: orography, exposure, wind fields, vegetation structure, land–atmosphere coupling, and drainage organisation.
A network that ignores these controls may perform well in purely statistical metrics while remaining blind to the mechanisms that govern hydrological response and transferability.

This workflow therefore treats station placement as a multi-layer abstraction problem, not as a single optimisation step.
Each modelling space reduces complexity while introducing explicit assumptions.

(Reference: modelling spaces S0–S5 and epistemic framing. :contentReferenceoaicite:4)

2 2. Observation as construction, not as given data (S1)

Spatial observations do not exist as neutral inputs.
Every dataset already encodes scale choices, sensor physics, preprocessing assumptions, and uncertainty structures.

In the Burgwald pipeline, S1 deliberately combines heterogeneous observation sources: terrain models, land-cover products, Sentinel-2 predictors, wind statistics, and hydrological derivatives.
The goal is not maximal completeness but process coverage.

This step establishes what the system can possibly learn — and what it fundamentally cannot.

3 3. From raw space to process proxies (S2)

Raw spatial data are not directly meaningful for design decisions. They must be transformed into descriptors that approximate dominant processes.

Examples: - Terrain derivatives approximate energy gradients, exposure, and flow potential. - Vegetation indices and classifications approximate canopy structure and interception potential. - Wind statistics approximate dominant atmospheric forcing directions. - Hydrological derivatives approximate connectivity and contributing area.

This transformation is not neutral:
each variable is a hypothesis about what matters for rainfall organisation and hydrological relevance.

Convenience layers (SAGA, CDSE, gdalcubes) are used deliberately to separate scientific intent from infrastructural overhead.
They do not simplify the science — they stabilise the workflow. :contentReferenceoaicite:5

4 4. Why segmentation precedes optimisation (S3)

Optimising directly in pixel space or catchment space embeds hidden assumptions about scale and homogeneity.

Segments are introduced as intermediate spatial units: - internally coherent, - externally distinct, - reproducible under parameter variation.

Segmentation is treated as a scale-discovery problem, not as classification.
Stability, plausibility and interpretability dominate over algorithmic optimality.

This aligns segmentation with functional unit concepts in hydrology and landscape ecology, while remaining data-driven. :contentReferenceoaicite:6

Segments become the common language between physical structure and decision logic.

5 5. Signatures, tokens, and explicit abstraction (S4)

Each segment is described through multiple domain signatures: physiography, hydrology, land cover, vegetation structure, and information-theoretic metrics.

These continuous variables are intentionally discretised into quantile-based bins (1/2/3).
The goal is not prediction accuracy but comparability and transparency.

Tokens encode combinations of process settings: they allow reasoning about coverage, redundancy, and representativeness without collapsing back into raw numerics.

This step transforms spatial heterogeneity into a structured decision space.

6 6. Decision logic: representativeness before efficiency

The network design follows a layered rationale: 1. Structural representativeness (physiographic coverage), 2. Information efficiency (redundancy reduction), 3. Hydrological relevance (connectivity and interpretability).

Multi-criteria decision analysis does not replace scientific judgement — it externalises it.

Trade-offs become explicit, inspectable and debatable. :contentReferenceoaicite:7

7 7. Scientific recursion and validation (S5)

The workflow is intentionally recursive. Intermediate results are not treated as final truths but as hypotheses that must be confronted with: - sensitivity, - stability, - alternative abstractions, - and conceptual consistency.

Refinement often means removing complexity, not adding it. :contentReferenceoaicite:8

This closes the loop between method and meaning.

8 8. From segments to concrete station candidates

Only at the end are abstract segments translated into concrete site candidates. Field constraints, accessibility, telemetry and maintenance enter here — deliberately late, not early.

The workflow ensures that practical compromises do not silently override scientific structure.

9 9. What this workflow teaches

This pipeline is not primarily about software. It teaches: - how spatial abstraction shapes knowledge, - how scale choices constrain interpretation, - how reproducibility enables scientific critique, - and how design decisions remain accountable.

The code operationalises a reasoning chain — not the other way around.
